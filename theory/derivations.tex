\title{Potential vorticity on a rotating sphere:\\
wave propagation and shear instability as one mechanism}
\author{Palaash Gang}
\date{}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
\noindent
The entire physics of this project is a corollary of one statement: material
conservation of potential vorticity on a rotating, curved surface,
$\D q/\D t = 0$ with $q = (\zeta+f)/h$. A fluid column carries $q$ as a label.
Displace it poleward and the planetary vorticity $f$ it feels grows, so its
relative vorticity $\zeta$ must fall to compensate; the circulation that
compensation demands is what carries a Rossby wave westward relative to the
fluid. Curve a zonal jet sharply enough that its own vorticity gradient locally
cancels the planetary one, and $q$ can no longer act as a single-signed
restoring agent across the jet; that loss of monotonicity is what permits
instability. These are not two topics. They are one mechanism examined in two
regimes, and the closing section makes the identification explicit: the wave and
the instability are the same physics, seen as one potential-vorticity interface
versus two.
\end{abstract}

\section{Physical setting and governing principle}
\label{sec:setting}

\subsection{The model and what it stands for}

The system studied throughout is the rotating shallow-water system on a sphere
of radius $R$ turning at rate $\Omega$: a single layer of homogeneous,
incompressible, inviscid fluid of thickness $h(\lambda,\varphi,t)$ with a free
upper surface, hydrostatic in the vertical and with horizontal velocity
independent of height. Here $\lambda$ is longitude and $\varphi$ latitude.

This is not a model of the atmosphere's vertical structure, and it is not
offered as one. It is a model of the \emph{barotropic response} of a stably
stratified layer: the depth-independent part of the horizontal flow, whose
dynamics are governed by horizontal advection of vorticity and by the stretching
and squashing of vortex columns. The free surface stands in for the vertical
displacement of an interior isopycnal or isentropic surface, and the layer depth
$h$ stands in for the mass between two such surfaces. That correspondence is why
a single-layer calculation can say something about a stratified atmosphere at
all, and it is also the boundary of what this project claims: nothing here
describes baroclinic conversion, which requires at least two degrees of freedom
in the vertical.

\subsection{The governing principle, before any coordinates}

The organising principle is stated once, physically, before any coordinate
machinery appears, because a reader should understand \emph{why} the quantity is
conserved before seeing it written in spherical coordinates.

Consider a material column of fluid: a small cylinder of cross-sectional area
$\delta A$ and height $h$, whose boundary is made of fluid particles and which
therefore deforms with the flow. Two conservation statements apply to it.

\emph{First}, Kelvin's circulation theorem. For a barotropic, inviscid fluid
under conservative body forces, the circulation of the \emph{absolute} velocity
around any material circuit is constant following the motion. Applying Stokes's
theorem to the circuit bounding $\delta A$, the absolute circulation is the flux
of absolute vorticity through it, so
%
\begin{equation}
\frac{\D}{\D t}\Big[ (\zeta + f)\,\delta A \Big] = 0 ,
\label{eq:kelvin}
\end{equation}
%
where $\zeta$ is the vertical component of relative vorticity and
$f = 2\Omega\sin\varphi$ is the planetary vorticity --- the vertical component of
twice the rotation vector, that is, the vorticity the fluid already has by
virtue of sitting on a rotating planet.

\emph{Second}, mass conservation. The column's mass $\rho\, h\, \delta A$ is
constant and $\rho$ is constant, so
%
\begin{equation}
\frac{\D}{\D t}\Big[ h \,\delta A \Big] = 0 .
\label{eq:masscolumn}
\end{equation}

Dividing \eqref{eq:kelvin} by \eqref{eq:masscolumn} eliminates the unknown area
$\delta A$ and leaves
%
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\;\frac{\D q}{\D t} = 0 , \qquad q \equiv \frac{\zeta + f}{h}\;}
\label{eq:pv}
\end{equation}
%
the material conservation of \emph{potential vorticity}. The name is apt: $q$
measures the vorticity a column would have if it were brought to unit depth,
so it is the vorticity the column has \emph{in potential}, held partly in its
rotation and partly in its stretching.

Three consequences are worth having in mind from the outset, because every later
section is one of them worked out.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{$q$ is a material label.} A column can be stretched, squashed,
strained and advected, but it cannot change its $q$. Any change in $f$ it
experiences must be paid for out of $\zeta$ or out of $h$.
\item \textbf{Displacement costs vorticity.} Moving poleward raises $f$. If $h$
is held fixed --- if the surface is stiff --- then $\zeta$ must fall by exactly
the same amount. This is the restoring mechanism of Section~\ref{sec:rest}.
\item \textbf{Monotonicity of the background $q$ is what makes it restoring.} If
the background $q$ increases poleward everywhere, a displaced column always
acquires a compensating vorticity of the same sign, and the disturbance
propagates. If the background $q$ has an extremum in the interior, that is no
longer true, and the disturbance can grow. This is
Section~\ref{sec:rayleighkuo}.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\emph{Physical payoff:} everything that follows is a consequence of one
conservation law, not a list of separate results.

\section{The rotating sphere as a curved manifold}
\label{sec:geometry}

The sphere is curved, and that is a physical fact, not a notational
inconvenience. A column travelling due east holds its heading yet is
continuously changing direction in the ambient space; the momentum equations
must account for that turning. The differential geometry introduced here is
exactly the bookkeeping this forces, and nothing more --- it is developed
because the physics demands it, not pursued for its own sake. Its payoff is that
it lets us say ``westward'' and ``the beta effect'' and mean physical statements
rather than accidents of a coordinate choice.

\subsection{Metric}

Arc length on the sphere is
%
\begin{equation}
\dd s^2 = R^2\,\dd\varphi^2 + R^2\cos^2\!\varphi\;\dd\lambda^2 ,
\label{eq:metric}
\end{equation}
%
so with $x^1 = \lambda$, $x^2 = \varphi$ the metric tensor is diagonal,
$g_{\lambda\lambda} = R^2\cos^2\!\varphi$, $g_{\varphi\varphi} = R^2$, and
$\sqrt{g} = R^2\cos\varphi$. The single factor $\cos\varphi$ is the whole story:
latitude circles shrink toward the poles, and every metric term below traces
back to that.

Throughout we use \emph{physical} (orthonormal) velocity components,
%
\begin{equation}
u = R\cos\varphi\,\frac{\D\lambda}{\D t}, \qquad
v = R\,\frac{\D\varphi}{\D t} ,
\label{eq:physcomp}
\end{equation}
%
which are the eastward and northward speeds an observer would measure, in
metres per second, at the point in question.

\subsection{Christoffel symbols}

From \eqref{eq:metric}, the connection coefficients
$\Gamma^k_{\;ij} = \tfrac12 g^{kl}(\partial_i g_{jl} + \partial_j g_{il} -
\partial_l g_{ij})$ have exactly two independent non-zero components,
%
\begin{equation}
\Gamma^{\lambda}_{\;\lambda\varphi} = \Gamma^{\lambda}_{\;\varphi\lambda}
   = -\tan\varphi ,
\qquad
\Gamma^{\varphi}_{\;\lambda\lambda} = \sin\varphi\cos\varphi ,
\label{eq:christoffel}
\end{equation}
%
all others vanishing. These are the connection coefficients of the $2$-sphere,
written in latitude rather than colatitude. They are not quoted from anywhere:
\file{check_christoffel_symbols.py} derives them from \eqref{eq:metric} by the
standard formula, then confirms them two further ways that need no external
source at all --- by recomputing the connection independently in the colatitude
chart and transporting it through $\varphi = \pi/2 - \theta$, and by checking
that the Gaussian curvature it implies is $1/R^2$, which a wrong connection
could not reproduce.

\subsection{What the symbols do}

The covariant material derivative of the velocity generates, in physical
components, the two familiar \emph{metric terms}
%
\begin{equation}
\Big(\frac{\D u}{\D t}\Big)_{\!\text{covariant}}
 = \frac{\D u}{\D t} - \frac{u v \tan\varphi}{R},
\qquad
\Big(\frac{\D v}{\D t}\Big)_{\!\text{covariant}}
 = \frac{\D v}{\D t} + \frac{u^2 \tan\varphi}{R} ,
\label{eq:metricterms}
\end{equation}
%
where on the right $\D/\D t = \partial_t + (u/R\cos\varphi)\partial_\lambda +
(v/R)\partial_\varphi$. The second term in each is the apparent force a column
feels because its local east and north directions rotate as it moves --- the
same effect that makes a great circle look curved on a Mercator chart.

The differential operators needed below follow from $\sqrt{g}$ in the standard
way, and in physical components read
%
\begin{align}
\grad\!\cdot\uvec &= \frac{1}{R\cos\varphi}
   \left[\frac{\partial u}{\partial\lambda}
       + \frac{\partial (v\cos\varphi)}{\partial\varphi}\right],
\label{eq:div}\\[2pt]
\zeta \;=\; \kvec\cdot\grad\!\times\uvec &= \frac{1}{R\cos\varphi}
   \left[\frac{\partial v}{\partial\lambda}
       - \frac{\partial (u\cos\varphi)}{\partial\varphi}\right],
\label{eq:curl}\\[2pt]
\nabla^2 \psi &= \frac{1}{R^2\cos^2\!\varphi}
      \frac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial\lambda^2}
   + \frac{1}{R^2\cos\varphi}\frac{\partial}{\partial\varphi}
     \left(\cos\varphi\,\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\varphi}\right).
\label{eq:lap}
\end{align}

The eigenfunctions of \eqref{eq:lap} are the surface spherical harmonics
$Y_n^m(\lambda,\varphi) = P_n^m(\sin\varphi)\,e^{i m\lambda}$, with
%
\begin{equation}
\nabla^2 Y_n^m = -\frac{n(n+1)}{R^2}\, Y_n^m .
\label{eq:lapeig}
\end{equation}
%
The quantity $n(n+1)/R^2$ is the squared total wavenumber of a mode on a sphere
of radius $R$ --- the spherical replacement for a plane wave's $k^2 + l^2$. Modes
of the same degree $n$ share a horizontal scale however that scale is divided
between the zonal and meridional directions, and this is the reason the phase
speed derived in Section~\ref{sec:rest} depends on $n$ alone.

\medskip
\noindent\emph{Physical payoff:} the metric is why ``westward'' and ``the beta
effect'' are not artefacts of a coordinate choice but real consequences of the
sphere's curvature. The factor $\cos\varphi$ in \eqref{eq:metric} is the same
factor that appears in $\beta$, and Section~\ref{sec:rest} shows the two cancel
exactly.

\section{The shallow-water equations and potential vorticity on the sphere}
\label{sec:swe}

\subsection{Vector-invariant momentum and continuity}

Written in vector-invariant form --- the form in which the rotational and
kinetic contributions are separated and no metric term appears explicitly,
because the vorticity carries them --- the inviscid shallow-water momentum
equation over a flat bottom is
%
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial\uvec}{\partial t}
 + (\zeta + f)\,\kvec\times\uvec
 + \grad\!\left(g h + \tfrac12|\uvec|^2\right) = 0 ,
\label{eq:mom}
\end{equation}
%
and mass conservation for the free-surface height is
%
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial h}{\partial t} + \grad\!\cdot(h\uvec) = 0 .
\label{eq:cont}
\end{equation}

In the physical components \eqref{eq:physcomp}, with
$B \equiv g h + \tfrac12(u^2+v^2)$ the Bernoulli function, these are
%
\begin{align}
\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} - (\zeta+f)\,v
  + \frac{1}{R\cos\varphi}\frac{\partial B}{\partial\lambda} &= 0 ,
\label{eq:momu}\\
\frac{\partial v}{\partial t} + (\zeta+f)\,u
  + \frac{1}{R}\frac{\partial B}{\partial\varphi} &= 0 ,
\label{eq:momv}\\
\frac{\partial h}{\partial t} + \frac{1}{R\cos\varphi}
  \left[\frac{\partial (h u)}{\partial\lambda}
      + \frac{\partial (h v\cos\varphi)}{\partial\varphi}\right] &= 0 ,
\label{eq:conth}
\end{align}
%
with $\zeta$ given by \eqref{eq:curl}. The metric terms
\eqref{eq:metricterms} are not absent; they have been absorbed into $\zeta$ and
into $\grad B$, which is precisely the economy the vector-invariant form buys.

\subsection{Vorticity}

Taking $\kvec\cdot\grad\times$ of \eqref{eq:mom}, the gradient term drops and the
identity $\kvec\cdot\grad\times(A\,\kvec\times\uvec) = \grad\!\cdot(A\uvec)$
turns the Coriolis term into a flux divergence:
%
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial \zeta}{\partial t}
 + \grad\!\cdot\big[(\zeta+f)\,\uvec\big] = 0 .
\label{eq:vort}
\end{equation}
%
This is already a strong statement: absolute vorticity obeys a conservation law
in flux form, so the only way to change the absolute vorticity of a region is to
carry it across the boundary. Expanding the divergence and using
$\D f/\D t = v\,\partial f/\partial y$ gives the advective form
%
\begin{equation}
\frac{\D(\zeta+f)}{\D t} = -(\zeta+f)\,\grad\!\cdot\uvec .
\label{eq:vortadv}
\end{equation}

\subsection{The reduction to $\D q/\D t = 0$}

Continuity \eqref{eq:cont} in advective form is
$\D h/\D t = -h\,\grad\!\cdot\uvec$, so the divergence can be eliminated between
the two:
%
\begin{equation}
\frac{1}{\zeta+f}\frac{\D(\zeta+f)}{\D t} = \frac{1}{h}\frac{\D h}{\D t}
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
\frac{\D}{\D t}\!\left(\frac{\zeta+f}{h}\right) = 0 ,
\label{eq:pvsphere}
\end{equation}
%
recovering \eqref{eq:pv} on the sphere, rigorously, using the operators
\eqref{eq:div}--\eqref{eq:curl} of Section~\ref{sec:geometry}.

It is worth recording the reduction in the sharper form that
\file{check_pv_conservation.py} verifies symbolically. Let $\boldsymbol{M}$
denote the residual of the momentum equations \eqref{eq:momu}--\eqref{eq:momv}
and $C$ the residual of \eqref{eq:conth}, for \emph{arbitrary} fields $u,v,h$
not assumed to satisfy anything. Then the exact algebraic identity
%
\begin{equation}
h\,\frac{\D q}{\D t}
 \;=\; \kvec\cdot\grad\!\times\boldsymbol{M} \;-\; q\,C
\label{eq:pvidentity}
\end{equation}
%
holds identically on the sphere. Setting $\boldsymbol{M}=0$ and $C=0$ --- that
is, imposing the equations of motion --- gives \eqref{eq:pvsphere} with no
residual term left over. The physical content of \eqref{eq:pvidentity} is that
potential-vorticity conservation is not an extra assumption bolted onto the
shallow-water system: it is what the curl of the momentum equation and the
continuity equation say jointly, and nothing else.

\medskip
\noindent\emph{Physical payoff:} \eqref{eq:pvsphere} is the equation every later
section is an application of.

\section{Nondimensionalisation}
\label{sec:nondim}

Two numbers control everything that follows, and their physical meanings must be
established before they are used.

Scale lengths by $R$, time by $(2\Omega)^{-1}$, velocity by a characteristic
speed $U$, and depth by a mean $H$. Two combinations appear:
%
\begin{equation}
\Ro \equiv \frac{U}{2\Omega R} ,
\qquad
\varepsilon \equiv \frac{4\Omega^2R^2}{gH} = \left(\frac{R}{L_d}\right)^{\!2} ,
\qquad
L_d \equiv \frac{\sqrt{gH}}{2\Omega} .
\label{eq:numbers}
\end{equation}
%
The first is the Rossby number; the second is Lamb's parameter, the reciprocal
of the Burger number, and it is the squared ratio of the planetary radius to the
external deformation radius $L_d$.

Writing $h = H\,(1 + \Ro\,\varepsilon\,\eta)$ --- the scaling that makes the
pressure gradient balance the Coriolis force at leading order --- the
nondimensional system is
%
\begin{align}
\frac{\partial\uvec}{\partial t}
  + \big(\Ro\,\zeta + \sin\varphi\big)\,\kvec\times\uvec
  + \Ro\,\grad\big(\tfrac12|\uvec|^2\big) + \grad\eta &= 0 ,
\label{eq:ndmom}\\[2pt]
\varepsilon\,\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial t}
  + \grad\!\cdot\Big[\big(1 + \Ro\,\varepsilon\,\eta\big)\uvec\Big] &= 0 ,
\label{eq:ndcont}
\end{align}
%
with all symbols now nondimensional, and the potential vorticity
%
\begin{equation}
q = \frac{\Ro\,\zeta + \sin\varphi}{1 + \Ro\,\varepsilon\,\eta} .
\label{eq:ndpv}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{What $\Ro$ controls.} The Rossby number sets the relative importance
of the fluid's own vorticity against the planetary background. It multiplies
$\zeta$ in \eqref{eq:ndpv}: when $\Ro \ll 1$ the potential-vorticity budget is
dominated by $f$, and the flow's own circulation is a perturbation to the
planet's. All the wave calculations of Sections~\ref{sec:rest}
and~\ref{sec:hough} are the $\Ro \to 0$ limit.

\paragraph{What $\varepsilon$ controls.} Lamb's parameter appears in exactly one
place, and it is the informative place: in \eqref{eq:ndcont} it multiplies the
surface-height tendency. It therefore measures how ``stiff'' the free surface is
against stretching --- how much of the potential-vorticity budget stretching can
absorb, versus how much must be carried by changes in relative vorticity. Read
the limits directly off \eqref{eq:ndcont}:
%
\begin{itemize}
\item $\varepsilon \to 0$ ($L_d \gg R$): the surface tendency drops out of the
continuity equation altogether, forcing $\grad\!\cdot\uvec \to 0$. The surface
behaves rigidly, columns cannot stretch, and \emph{every} change in $f$ a
displaced column experiences must be paid for out of $\zeta$. This is the
nondivergent limit, and it is where Section~\ref{sec:rest} lives.
\item $\varepsilon$ large ($L_d \ll R$): the surface is compliant, columns
stretch readily, and a growing share of the potential-vorticity budget is
satisfied by stretching rather than by relative vorticity. The restoring
mechanism is diluted and the wave slows.
\end{itemize}
%
For Earth-like values ($\Omega = 7.292\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$,
$R = 6.371\times10^{6}\,\mathrm{m}$, $g = 9.806\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$,
$H = 10\,\mathrm{km}$) one finds $\varepsilon \approx 8.80$, i.e.
$R/L_d \approx 2.97$: the planet is about three deformation radii across, so
the divergent correction is not a small perturbation and should be measurable.

\medskip
\noindent\emph{Physical payoff:} $\varepsilon$ is the single number that governs
the transition explored in Section~\ref{sec:hough}, and its physical meaning ---
the compliance of the free surface against vortex stretching --- is fixed here
before it is used.

\section{Linearisation I: waves on a state of rest}
\label{sec:rest}

\subsection{The nondivergent barotropic vorticity equation}

Take $\varepsilon\to 0$, so $\grad\!\cdot\uvec = 0$ and the flow derives from a
streamfunction, $\uvec = \kvec\times\grad\psi$, with
%
\begin{equation}
u = -\frac{1}{R}\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\varphi},
\qquad
v = \frac{1}{R\cos\varphi}\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\lambda},
\qquad
\zeta = \nabla^2\psi .
\label{eq:streamfn}
\end{equation}
%
Equation \eqref{eq:pvsphere} with $h$ constant reduces to material conservation
of \emph{absolute} vorticity, $\D(\zeta+f)/\D t = 0$. Linearising about a state
of rest --- discarding $\uvec\!\cdot\!\grad\zeta$, which is quadratic in the
disturbance --- leaves only the advection of planetary vorticity by the
disturbance's own meridional velocity:
%
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial (\nabla^2\psi)}{\partial t}
 + \frac{2\Omega}{R^2}\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\lambda} = 0 .
\label{eq:bve}
\end{equation}

The step from $v\,\partial f/\partial y$ to $(2\Omega/R^2)\,\partial_\lambda\psi$
is worth pausing on, because it is where the sphere earns its keep. The
planetary vorticity gradient is
%
\begin{equation}
\beta \equiv \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}
 = \frac{1}{R}\frac{\partial}{\partial\varphi}\big(2\Omega\sin\varphi\big)
 = \frac{2\Omega\cos\varphi}{R} ,
\label{eq:beta}
\end{equation}
%
and $v$ carries a factor $1/\cos\varphi$ from the metric \eqref{eq:streamfn}.
The two factors of $\cos\varphi$ cancel \emph{exactly}. This cancellation is the
reason the spherical phase speed below depends on the mode's degree alone and
carries no residual latitude dependence --- on a $\beta$-plane, where $\beta$ is
frozen at one latitude, that exactness is lost.

\subsection{Dispersion relation}

Substituting a spherical-harmonic normal mode
$\psi = \hat\psi\,P_n^m(\sin\varphi)\,e^{i(m\lambda - \omega t)}$ into
\eqref{eq:bve} and using \eqref{eq:lapeig} gives, after cancelling the common
factor,
%
\begin{equation}
\omega\, \frac{n(n+1)}{R^2} + \frac{2\Omega m}{R^2} = 0
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
\boxed{\;c_{\mathrm{ang}} \equiv \frac{\omega}{m}
      = -\,\frac{2\Omega}{n(n+1)}\;}
\label{eq:rh}
\end{equation}
%
the Rossby--Haurwitz angular phase speed \citep{rossby1939,haurwitz1940b}. The
corresponding zonal phase speed at latitude $\varphi$ is
%
\begin{equation}
c = c_{\mathrm{ang}}\,R\cos\varphi = -\,\frac{2\Omega R\cos\varphi}{n(n+1)} .
\label{eq:rhzonal}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Why westward, and why that is not a convention}

The sign in \eqref{eq:rh} is negative for every $n \ge 1$ and every $m$, and it
is derived, not chosen. The argument is short enough to give in full, and it is
the mechanism the whole project rests on.

Let a chain of fluid columns along a latitude circle be displaced meridionally by
$\eta(x) = \eta_0\sin kx$, with $x$ eastward and $\eta$ positive northward.
Conservation of $q$ with the depth held fixed --- the $\varepsilon\to0$
condition --- means the column's absolute vorticity is unchanged, so the
relative vorticity it acquires exactly offsets the planetary vorticity it has
gained:
%
\begin{equation}
\zeta' = -\beta\,\eta = -\beta\eta_0\sin kx .
\label{eq:zetaeta}
\end{equation}
%
Columns displaced northward turn anticyclonically; columns displaced southward
turn cyclonically. Inverting $\zeta' = \partial_x^2\psi'$ for this sinusoid gives
$\psi' = (\beta\eta_0/k^2)\sin kx$, and hence a meridional velocity
%
\begin{equation}
v' = \frac{\partial\psi'}{\partial x} = \frac{\beta\eta_0}{k}\cos kx ,
\label{eq:vinduced}
\end{equation}
%
which is a quarter wavelength out of phase with the displacement. That
quadrature is the crux: a velocity \emph{in} phase with the displacement would
make the pattern grow or decay in place, whereas a velocity in quadrature makes
it translate. Imposing the kinematic condition $\partial\eta/\partial t = v'$ on
$\eta = \eta_0\sin k(x - ct)$ gives
%
\begin{equation}
c = -\frac{\beta}{k^2} \;<\; 0 .
\label{eq:betaplane}
\end{equation}
%
Both $\beta$ and $k^2$ are positive, so $c$ is negative unconditionally. Reading
the mechanism off the algebra: the induced circulation pushes fluid northward
just \emph{west} of each northward crest and southward just west of each
southward trough, so the whole pattern is rebuilt one step to the west at each
instant. Nothing propagates east.

Equation \eqref{eq:betaplane} and the spherical result \eqref{eq:rhzonal} are the
same statement: substituting $\beta = 2\Omega\cos\varphi/R$ from \eqref{eq:beta}
and the spherical total wavenumber $K^2 = n(n+1)/R^2$ from \eqref{eq:lapeig}
into $c = -\beta/K^2$ returns \eqref{eq:rhzonal} identically.

\medskip
\noindent\emph{Physical payoff:} \eqref{eq:rh} is hypotheses H1 and H2 of the
project blueprint, and it is the nondivergent reference point against which
Section~\ref{sec:hough} measures a real physical departure.

\section{Linearisation II: the divergent correction --- Laplace's tidal
equations and Hough modes}
\label{sec:hough}

\subsection{Keeping the free surface}

Section~\ref{sec:rest} threw the free surface away. Restoring it changes the
physics, not merely the arithmetic. A column may now change its depth, so
material conservation of $q = (\zeta+f)/h$ no longer forces the entire change in
$f$ onto $\zeta$: part of it is absorbed by vortex stretching. The restoring
agent is diluted, and the wave must slow. Lamb's parameter $\varepsilon$ from
\eqref{eq:numbers} measures precisely how much of the budget the surface can
take.

Linearising \eqref{eq:momu}--\eqref{eq:conth} about rest with mean depth $H$,
writing $\eta$ for the surface displacement, and nondimensionalising as in
Section~\ref{sec:nondim} with $\tau = 2\Omega t$ and $\mu = \sin\varphi$, the
system becomes Laplace's tidal equations,
%
\begin{align}
\frac{\partial U}{\partial \tau} - \mu V
  &= -\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\mu^2}}\frac{\partial N}{\partial\lambda} ,
\label{eq:lte1}\\
\frac{\partial V}{\partial \tau} + \mu U
  &= -\sqrt{1-\mu^2}\;\frac{\partial N}{\partial\mu} ,
\label{eq:lte2}\\
\varepsilon\,\frac{\partial N}{\partial \tau}
  &+ \frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\mu^2}}\frac{\partial U}{\partial\lambda}
   + \frac{\partial}{\partial\mu}\Big[\sqrt{1-\mu^2}\,V\Big] = 0 ,
\label{eq:lte3}
\end{align}
%
where $U, V, N$ are the nondimensional eastward velocity, northward velocity and
surface height. The classical formulation and its eigenstructure are due to
\citet{longuethiggins1968}, with the vector-harmonic treatment of
\citet{swarztrauber1985}; the derivation here does not depend on either, since
the validation target is the internal $\varepsilon\to0$ limit rather than an
external published table (see Section~\ref{sec:hough:limit}).

Note where $\varepsilon$ sits: it multiplies $\partial N/\partial\tau$ in
\eqref{eq:lte3} and appears nowhere else. That single placement is the whole
physical content of the parameter.

\subsection{The eigenvalue problem, in a form that can be solved}

Eliminating $U$ and $V$ from \eqref{eq:lte1}--\eqref{eq:lte3} for a normal mode
$\propto e^{i(m\lambda - \sigma\tau)}$ yields the classical second-order
Laplace tidal equation for $N(\mu)$, which is a \emph{nonlinear} eigenvalue
problem in $\sigma$ and carries a coordinate singularity wherever
$\mu^2 = \sigma^2$. Both features make it awkward numerically and neither is
physical. We therefore keep the system in the vorticity--divergence--height
variables, in which it is linear in $\sigma$ and regular everywhere.

Writing $\zeta$, $\delta = \grad\!\cdot\uvec$ and $\eta$ and using the exact
operator identities
%
\begin{align}
\frac{\partial\zeta}{\partial t} &= -\grad\!\cdot(f\uvec) , &
\frac{\partial\delta}{\partial t} &=
  \kvec\cdot\grad\!\times(f\uvec) - g\nabla^2\eta , &
\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial t} &= -H\delta ,
\label{eq:zdh}
\end{align}
%
and expanding each field in surface spherical harmonics of fixed order $m$,
$\Lambda_n \equiv n(n+1)$, gives the linear eigenvalue problem
%
\begin{equation}
\frac{\dd}{\dd\tau}\!
\begin{pmatrix}\zeta_n\\ \delta_n\\ \tilde\eta_n\end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
 i m/\Lambda_n & -B & 0\\
 B & i m/\Lambda_n & \Lambda_n/\sqrt{\varepsilon}\\
 0 & -1/\sqrt{\varepsilon} & 0
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}\zeta_n\\ \delta_n\\ \tilde\eta_n\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
B = M - D\,\Lambda^{-1} ,
\label{eq:houghevp}
\end{equation}
%
with $\tilde\eta = \eta/(H\sqrt\varepsilon)$ a rescaling that keeps the matrix
well conditioned, and with the two Legendre coupling matrices
%
\begin{equation}
M_{nn'} = \big\langle P_n, \mu P_{n'}\big\rangle ,
\qquad
D_{nn'} = \Big\langle P_n, (1-\mu^2)\,\tfrac{\dd P_{n'}}{\dd\mu}\Big\rangle ,
\label{eq:legmat}
\end{equation}
%
both tridiagonal, since $\mu$ and $(1-\mu^2)\dd/\dd\mu$ couple degree $n$ only to
$n\pm1$. This is the specification Session~L5's \file{evp_hough.py}
implements.

\subsection{The $\varepsilon\to0$ limit, in closed form}
\label{sec:hough:limit}

Setting $\delta = \tilde\eta = 0$ in \eqref{eq:houghevp} --- which is exactly
what $\varepsilon\to0$ enforces, since \eqref{eq:ndcont} then forbids the surface
from storing any divergence --- leaves the diagonal system
$\dd\zeta_n/\dd\tau = (im/\Lambda_n)\zeta_n$, whose eigenvalues are
%
\begin{equation}
\sigma \;\longrightarrow\; -\,\frac{m}{n(n+1)}
\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad
c_{\mathrm{ang}} = 2\Omega\,\frac{\sigma}{m}
 \;\longrightarrow\; -\,\frac{2\Omega}{n(n+1)} ,
\label{eq:limit}
\end{equation}
%
recovering \eqref{eq:rh} exactly. This is the project's validation target for
extension~B, and it is stronger than an external table would be: a table
supplies a handful of numbers, whereas \eqref{eq:limit} is a closed form the
project derives independently, against which the full dispersion curve and
eigenmode structure can be checked at the project's own resolution and
parameters.

The approach to that limit is first order in $\varepsilon$.
\file{check_hough_epsilon_limit.py} solves \eqref{eq:houghevp} across
$\varepsilon \in [10^{-6}, 10^{-2}]$ and fits the log--log convergence rate,
obtaining $1.0000$ to four figures for every degree and order tested. First
order is what the physics predicts: the fraction of the potential-vorticity
budget the surface can absorb is proportional to its compliance, and the
compliance is $\varepsilon$ itself.

\subsection{What the free surface actually does, at Earth's $\varepsilon$}

At $\varepsilon \approx 8.80$ the correction is large, and it is a statement
about the mechanism rather than a residual error. Solving \eqref{eq:houghevp}
and following each Rossby branch by continuation from small $\varepsilon$ gives,
for example, at $m=1$: the $n=2$ mode slowed by $40\%$, $n=3$ by $28\%$, $n=5$
by $13\%$, $n=8$ by $6\%$. Every mode is slowed, and the fractional slowing
falls as $n$ rises.

The trend is the physics stated plainly. A large-scale mode has a horizontal
scale comparable to $L_d$, so the surface deflection needed to balance its flow
is a substantial fraction of the layer depth and a substantial share of the
potential-vorticity budget goes into stretching. A small-scale mode has a scale
far below $L_d$, the surface barely deflects, and the flow is effectively
nondivergent: it behaves as Section~\ref{sec:rest} says it should.

\subsection{The sectoral modes are a genuine special case}
\label{sec:hough:sectoral}

One family departs from that trend, systematically and for a structural reason
worth stating rather than smoothing over. The \emph{sectoral} modes, those with
$n = m$, are consistently slowed \emph{less} than their $n = m+1$ neighbours ---
at $m=2$, for instance, $7.5\%$ against $19.8\%$. This holds for every $m$
tested, so it is a property of the sectoral family and not an artefact of one
calculation.

The cause is visible in the structure of \eqref{eq:houghevp}. The Coriolis
coupling matrix $B$ is tridiagonal: multiplication by $\mu = \sin\varphi$ and by
$(1-\mu^2)\,\dd/\dd\mu$ each connect degree $n$ only to $n\pm1$. A sectoral mode
sits at the bottom of the degree ladder, since $n \ge m$ always, so it has only
\emph{one} neighbour to couple through instead of two. Physically: the rotation
of the sphere can hand a sectoral mode's rotational flow to one adjacent degree
rather than two, so a smaller share of its potential-vorticity budget is
available to be absorbed by vortex stretching, and it is correspondingly less
slowed by the free surface.

This is demonstrated causally rather than inferred. Deleting the lower
neighbour of a \emph{non}-sectoral mode from the spectral basis --- leaving its
latitudinal structure untouched --- collapses its slowing to sectoral values: at
$(m,n) = (2,3)$, from $19.8\%$ to $6.4\%$, against a true sectoral $7.5\%$.

A second, weaker effect points the same way. The relevant stiffness is set by
the \emph{local} deformation radius $\sqrt{gH}/|f|$, which grows toward the
equator, so the appropriate parameter for a given mode is
$\varepsilon\langle\sin^2\!\varphi\rangle/[n(n+1)]$ rather than $\varepsilon$
itself. Sectoral modes, whose associated Legendre function
$P_m^m \propto \cos^m\!\varphi$ has no meridional node and is equatorially
confined, sit where that local deformation radius is largest.

\medskip
\noindent\emph{Physical payoff:} this is hypothesis H5, expressed as what it
actually is --- a statement about how a free surface changes the restoring
mechanism --- rather than as a quantified departure from a reference curve. It
is also the single strongest upgrade available to this project: an exact,
internally validated target for how the free surface changes wave dispersion.

\section{The Rhines scale}
\label{sec:rhines}

\subsection{Where the linear theory stops}

Sections~\ref{sec:rest} and~\ref{sec:hough} discarded $\uvec\!\cdot\!\grad\zeta$
as quadratic. That term does not stay small. Its role is to move energy between
scales, and on a $\beta$-plane it competes directly with the linear restoring
term. The scale at which the two balance is where the character of the flow
changes.

Estimate both terms for a flow of characteristic speed $U$ and length $L$. The
relative vorticity is $\zeta \sim U/L$, so the nonlinear advection of vorticity
scales as
%
\begin{equation}
|\uvec\!\cdot\!\grad\zeta| \sim \frac{U^2}{L^2} ,
\qquad\text{while}\qquad
|\beta v| \sim \beta U .
\label{eq:rhinesscaling}
\end{equation}
%
Equating them gives the \emph{Rhines scale}
%
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\;L_R \sim \sqrt{\frac{U}{\beta}}\;}
\label{eq:rhines}
\end{equation}
%
which \citet{rhines1975} obtained as an arrest wavenumber
$k_\beta = (\beta/2U)^{1/2}$, i.e. $L_R = (2U/\beta)^{1/2}$ with the factor of
two made explicit. \citet{vallis1993} reach the same transition scale
$O(\sqrt{U/\beta})$ from the weakly nonlinear interaction of Rossby waves and
show that it is the anisotropy of that interaction, not the isotropic arrest
alone, that produces jets.

\subsection{What it means physically}

The identical balance can be read as a competition of rates. Two-dimensional
turbulence cascades energy \emph{upscale}, so eddies grow. As they grow, the
Rossby-wave frequency $\omega = \beta k_x/K^2$ available at their scale rises
relative to their eddy turnover rate $UK$. Once wave propagation is faster than
eddy turnover, a disturbance disperses as a wave before the nonlinearity can
reorganise it, and the upscale cascade is arrested. That happens at
$K \sim \sqrt{\beta/U}$, which is \eqref{eq:rhines} again.

Crucially, the arrest is \emph{anisotropic}. The restoring term $\beta v$ acts
only on meridional motion; a purely zonal flow has $v = 0$ and feels no
restoring force at all. So the cascade is halted in the meridional direction
while remaining free in the zonal one, and the energy piles up in zonally
elongated structures. This is why $L_R$ separates isotropic turbulence from
planetary banding: it is the scale at which the beta effect starts vetoing
meridional rearrangement but not zonal.

\subsection{Consequence for the rotation-rate campaign}

Because $\beta = 2\Omega\cos\varphi/R$ is proportional to $\Omega$,
\eqref{eq:rhines} gives
%
\begin{equation}
L_R \;\propto\; \Omega^{-1/2} .
\label{eq:rhinesomega}
\end{equation}
%
The blueprint's phase-speed campaign sweeps $\Omega$ over a sixteen-fold range
($0.25\,\Omega_0$ to $4\,\Omega_0$, runs P-08 through P-12), which sweeps $L_R$
by a factor of $\sqrt{16} = 4$. That reframes the sweep. It is not a
numerical-convergence exercise with $\Omega$ as a knob; it is a survey of
planetary regime. A four-fold change in the scale at which an atmosphere
organises itself is the difference between a few broad jets and many narrow
ones, which is the difference between a terrestrial banding pattern and a Jovian
one.

\medskip
\noindent\emph{Physical payoff:} this gives the discussion section somewhere real
to go --- Jovian banding, slowly rotating worlds --- rather than trailing into
generic future work.

\section{Instability of a zonal jet: the Rayleigh--Kuo criterion}
\label{sec:rayleighkuo}

\subsection{Linearisation about a zonal base state}

Now linearise about a zonal flow $\bar u(\varphi)$ rather than about rest. Its
relative vorticity and the background absolute vorticity are
%
\begin{equation}
\bar\zeta = -\frac{1}{R\cos\varphi}
   \frac{\dd(\bar u\cos\varphi)}{\dd\varphi} ,
\qquad
Q(\varphi) = \bar\zeta + f = \bar\zeta + 2\Omega\sin\varphi ,
\label{eq:basestate}
\end{equation}
%
so the background potential-vorticity gradient on the sphere is
%
\begin{equation}
\frac{\dd Q}{\dd y} = \frac{1}{R}\frac{\dd Q}{\dd\varphi}
= \underbrace{\frac{2\Omega\cos\varphi}{R}}_{\textstyle \beta}
 \;-\; \frac{1}{R}\frac{\dd}{\dd\varphi}\!
   \left[\frac{1}{R\cos\varphi}
   \frac{\dd(\bar u\cos\varphi)}{\dd\varphi}\right] .
\label{eq:dQdy}
\end{equation}
%
On a flat plane the second term collapses to $\dd^2\bar u/\dd y^2$ and
\eqref{eq:dQdy} becomes the familiar $\beta - \bar u_{yy}$. On the sphere it does
not: the $\cos\varphi$ factors from the metric \eqref{eq:metric} survive and the
curvature term is not a bare second derivative. This is the first of the two
places the sphere enters, and it is quantitative rather than structural --- it
changes \emph{where} the criterion is met, not \emph{whether} the argument works.

The linearised potential-vorticity equation for a disturbance streamfunction
$\psi'$ is then
%
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial\zeta'}{\partial t}
 + \bar u_a\,\frac{\partial\zeta'}{\partial\lambda}
 + \frac{1}{R^2\cos\varphi}\frac{\dd Q}{\dd\varphi}\,
   \frac{\partial\psi'}{\partial\lambda} = 0 ,
\qquad
\bar u_a \equiv \frac{\bar u}{R\cos\varphi} ,
\label{eq:linpv}
\end{equation}
%
where $\bar u_a$ is the base flow's \emph{angular} velocity. On a sphere the
natural phase speed is angular, because a wave of fixed zonal wavenumber sweeps
out equal angles, not equal distances, at different latitudes.

\subsection{The necessary condition}

Substitute the normal mode
$\psi' = \Psi(\varphi)\,e^{i m(\lambda - c_a t)}$, with $c_a = c_r + i c_i$ the
complex angular phase speed. Dividing out the common factor $i m$ gives the
governing ordinary differential equation
%
\begin{equation}
\big(\bar u_a - c_a\big)\,\mathcal{L}_m\Psi
 + \frac{1}{R^2\cos\varphi}\frac{\dd Q}{\dd\varphi}\,\Psi = 0 ,
\label{eq:evp}
\end{equation}
%
where $\mathcal{L}_m$ is the spherical Laplacian \eqref{eq:lap} at zonal order
$m$,
%
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_m\Psi = \frac{1}{R^2\cos\varphi}
   \frac{\dd}{\dd\varphi}\!\left(\cos\varphi\,\frac{\dd\Psi}{\dd\varphi}\right)
 - \frac{m^2}{R^2\cos^2\!\varphi}\,\Psi .
\label{eq:Lm}
\end{equation}

Multiply \eqref{eq:evp} by $\Psi^{*}$, divide by $(\bar u_a - c_a)$, and
integrate over latitude with the sphere's own measure $\cos\varphi\,\dd\varphi$.
The left-hand term is handled by the pointwise identity
%
\begin{equation}
\Psi^{*}\mathcal{L}_m\Psi\,\cos\varphi
= \frac{1}{R^2}\left\{
  \frac{\dd}{\dd\varphi}\Big[\cos\varphi\,\Psi^{*}\Psi_\varphi\Big]
  - \cos\varphi\,|\Psi_\varphi|^2
  - \frac{m^2|\Psi|^2}{\cos\varphi}\right\} .
\label{eq:byparts}
\end{equation}
%
This is the second place the sphere enters, and here it changes nothing
structural. Regularity of $\Psi$ at both poles makes the exact-derivative term
integrate to zero --- exactly the role that no-normal-flow walls play in the
flat-channel argument --- and the two remaining terms are manifestly real and
non-negative. Hence
%
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{A} \equiv \int \Psi^{*}\mathcal{L}_m\Psi\,\cos\varphi\,\dd\varphi
\qquad\text{is real and negative.}
\label{eq:Areal}
\end{equation}

But \eqref{eq:evp} also gives
$\mathcal{L}_m\Psi = -\,\big(\dd Q/\dd\varphi\big)\Psi
/\big[R^2\cos\varphi\,(\bar u_a - c_a)\big]$, so, the $\cos\varphi$ cancelling,
%
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{A} = -\frac{1}{R^2}\int
  \frac{\dd Q}{\dd\varphi}\,
  \frac{|\Psi|^2}{\bar u_a - c_a}\,\dd\varphi .
\label{eq:Asecond}
\end{equation}
%
Since $\mathcal{A}$ is real, the imaginary part of the right-hand side must
vanish. Using
$\operatorname{Im}\big[(\bar u_a - c_a)^{-1}\big]
 = c_i/|\bar u_a - c_a|^2$,
%
\begin{equation}
c_i \int \frac{\dd Q}{\dd\varphi}\,
   \frac{|\Psi|^2}{|\bar u_a - c_a|^2}\,\dd\varphi = 0 .
\label{eq:rk}
\end{equation}
%
The weight $|\Psi|^2/|\bar u_a - c_a|^2$ is non-negative and not identically
zero. Therefore, for a growing mode ($c_i \neq 0$), the integral can vanish only
if $\dd Q/\dd\varphi$ --- equivalently $\dd Q/\dd y$ --- \textbf{changes sign
somewhere in the domain}. This is the Rayleigh--Kuo criterion \citep{kuo1949},
in the form adapted to the sphere.

The assumptions used are exactly these, and no others: the base flow is real and
zonal; $\Psi$ is regular at both poles; $\Psi$ is not identically zero; the
integrals converge.

\subsection{Necessary, not sufficient}

\textbf{This is a necessary condition, not a sufficient one.} A base state
satisfying it may still be perfectly stable. The argument above establishes only
that a growing mode \emph{requires} a sign change; it says nothing about whether
any growing mode exists, what its growth rate would be, or at which zonal
wavenumber it would appear. Read as a prohibition it is powerful --- no sign
change, no instability, full stop, which is hypothesis H7 of the blueprint and
the strongest test in the set because it forbids rather than fits. Read as a
prediction of instability it says nothing at all.

Section~\ref{sec:evp} is what converts it into an actual computational test.

\medskip
\noindent\emph{Physical payoff:} this is the diagnostic already used
predictively in the project's Phase-0 gate, which identified $32$--$58^\circ$N as
the band where the Galewsky jet meets the criterion, before any simulation was
run --- closing the loop between an analytic criterion and an observed outcome.

\section{The linear stability eigenvalue problem}
\label{sec:evp}

\subsection{Statement of the problem}

Equation \eqref{eq:evp} is already an eigenvalue problem; it needs only to be
posed as one. Rearranged,
%
\begin{equation}
\underbrace{\left[\bar u_a\,\mathcal{L}_m
  + \frac{1}{R^2\cos\varphi}\frac{\dd Q}{\dd\varphi}\right]}_{\textstyle \mathcal{A}_m}
  \Psi
\;=\; c_a \underbrace{\mathcal{L}_m}_{\textstyle \mathcal{B}_m}\Psi ,
\label{eq:genevp}
\end{equation}
%
a generalised eigenvalue problem for the complex angular phase speed $c_a$ at
each zonal wavenumber $m$. The growth rate of the mode is
%
\begin{equation}
\sigma(m) = m\,\operatorname{Im}(c_a) ,
\label{eq:growth}
\end{equation}
%
in inverse seconds, and its eigenfunction $\Psi(\varphi)$ is the meridional
structure the instability will take.

\subsection{Regularity, discretisation, and the critical layer}

\paragraph{Boundary conditions.} There are no boundaries. The sphere is closed,
and what replaces boundary conditions is \emph{regularity at the poles}: $\Psi$
must be single-valued and finite as $\varphi \to \pm\pi/2$, which for $m \neq 0$
requires $\Psi \to 0$ there. The clean way to impose this is not to impose it at
all but to expand $\Psi$ in surface spherical harmonics of order $m$,
%
\begin{equation}
\Psi(\varphi) = \sum_{n=m}^{N} a_n\,P_n^m(\sin\varphi) ,
\label{eq:expansion}
\end{equation}
%
since every basis function is regular at the poles by construction. In this
basis $\mathcal{B}_m$ is diagonal, $(\mathcal{B}_m)_{nn} = -\Lambda_n/R^2$, and
$\mathcal{A}_m$ requires only the two multiplication matrices
$\langle P_{n'}, \bar u_a P_n\rangle$ and
$\langle P_{n'}, W P_n\rangle$ with
$W \equiv (\dd Q/\dd\varphi)/(R^2\cos\varphi)$, both computed by Gauss--Legendre
quadrature in $\mu = \sin\varphi$. Note that $W$ is regular at the poles despite
appearances, because $\dd Q/\dd\varphi \to 2\Omega\cos\varphi$ there for any jet
of compact support.

\paragraph{The critical layer.} Where $\bar u_a(\varphi_c) = c_r$, the
coefficient of the highest derivative in \eqref{eq:evp} vanishes. Physically
this is the latitude at which the base flow's angular velocity matches the
mode's --- where a fluid column and the wave pattern travel together, so the
column feels a steady rather than an oscillating forcing and can be displaced
without limit. For a genuinely growing mode $c_i \neq 0$ and
$\bar u_a - c_a$ never vanishes on the real axis, so the operator is regular and
the discretisation is unproblematic. For a \emph{neutral} mode the singularity is
real, one of the two solutions acquires a logarithmic branch point, and the
resolution requires either the viscous indentation of \citet{kuo1949} or an
explicit contour deformation. \citet{bretherton1966} showed that this is not a
technicality peripheral to instability but central to it: a critical layer must
be present for a growing normal mode of a general zonal flow, and it carries a
down-gradient flux of potential vorticity that cannot vanish as the growth rate
does.

\paragraph{Spurious modes.} A finite discretisation of a problem with a
continuous spectrum always produces eigenvalues that are artefacts of the grid,
and for a barotropic jet these masquerade as weakly growing modes. The filter is
resolution doubling: solve at truncation $N$ and at $2N$ and retain only those
eigenvalues that do not move. \file{check_rayleigh_kuo.py} applies exactly
this test at $N = 240$ and $N = 480$ and finds a clean separation --- growth
rates for $m \le 8$ agree to better than $2\times10^{-4}$ relative, while for
$m \ge 9$ they move by $10^{-3}$ or more and are discarded.

\subsection{What it delivers, on the Galewsky jet}

Solving \eqref{eq:genevp} for the Galewsky jet gives a resolved unstable band
$1 \le m \le 8$ peaking at
%
\begin{equation}
m^{*} \approx 6 , \qquad
\sigma(m^{*}) = 2.07\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}},
\qquad
\text{$e$-folding time } 0.56\ \text{days} .
\label{eq:galewskygrowth}
\end{equation}
%
The approximation sign is meant literally, and the reason is more serious than
numerical scatter.

The eigenvalue problem \eqref{eq:genevp} is posed for the \emph{nondivergent}
barotropic potential-vorticity equation, while the jet it describes lives in the
divergent shallow-water system, whose external deformation radius
$L_d = \sqrt{gH}/2\Omega \approx 3\times10^{6}\ \mathrm{m}$ is comparable to the
jet's own width. That is not a detail. \citet{paldor2020} compare
barotropic-instability growth rates for a zonal jet on a sphere across the
nondivergent, quasi-geostrophic and full shallow-water formulations, and report
that at mean depths of $5$--$10\ \mathrm{km}$ the shallow-water growth rates can
be smaller than the nondivergent ones by more than $50\%$, the three
formulations converging only above about $30\ \mathrm{km}$. This project uses
$H = 10\ \mathrm{km}$, inside the band where that discrepancy is largest.

Two consequences follow, and they govern how \eqref{eq:galewskygrowth} may be
read. First, $\sigma$ is a \emph{nondivergent modal} growth rate, and carries a
formulation-level bias of order tens of per cent relative to the shallow-water
system the nonlinear runs integrate. Second, and because of the first, the
apparent resolution of the calculation is illusory below that bias: the raw
values at $m = 6$ and $m = 7$ differ by about $0.07\%$, but discriminating two
wavenumbers at sub-per-cent separation inside a bias two orders of magnitude
larger is not a physical claim. It is arithmetic on a biased operator.

What survives is coarser and genuinely robust: growth is resolved for
$1 \le m \le 8$, the peak sits on a broad \emph{plateau} spanning $m = 6$--$7$,
and the $e$-folding time is of order half a day rather than days or hours. Those
are the statements the nonlinear runs can be asked to reproduce.

By contrast, solid-body rotation --- whose $\dd Q/\dd y = 2(\Omega + U_0/R)
\cos\varphi$ has no sign change --- yields a spectrum that is real to machine
precision at every $m$ tested. The two results together are the content of the
previous section's caveat: the criterion forbids, but only the solved eigenvalue
problem predicts.

\medskip
\noindent\emph{Physical payoff:} this converts Section~\ref{sec:rayleighkuo}'s
one-sided necessary condition into an actual prediction --- growth rates and
eigenmode structure, not merely a permission to grow --- closing the gap the
blueprint flags.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{One caveat, stated because it is easy to overstate this
section.} A normal-mode eigenvalue problem is \emph{not} a sufficient condition
for stability. The linearised operator here is non-normal: its eigenfunctions
are not orthogonal, so a superposition of individually decaying modes can
amplify substantially before it decays. A spectrum with no growing eigenvalue
therefore establishes asymptotic stability to infinitesimal normal-mode
disturbances, and nothing more --- it does not exclude finite-time transient
growth, which in shear flows can be large. What Section~\ref{sec:evp} supplies
is the \emph{modal} growth rate, which is what hypotheses H7--H9 are about and
what the nonlinear runs are validated against. Transient, non-modal growth is
outside the scope of this project and is not claimed to be excluded.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{What the divergent system would require instead.}
\citet{ripa1983} gives sufficient conditions for the stability of a zonal flow in
a one-layer model on the $\beta$-plane or the sphere --- without the
quasi-geostrophic approximation and, unlike the present analysis, without
restriction to normal modes. Two conditions must hold together: that the product
of the meridional potential-vorticity gradient with the difference between an
arbitrary constant and the zonal velocity be everywhere non-negative, which is
the Fj\o{}rtoft-strengthened form of Section~\ref{sec:rayleighkuo}'s criterion;
and that the magnitude of that same difference nowhere exceed the local speed of
long gravity waves. The second has no nondivergent analogue --- it is a
criticality condition, and it disappears in the rigid-lid limit where
$\sqrt{gH}\to\infty$. So divergence does not \emph{modify} the classical
condition; it \emph{adds} one. For the jet studied here that added condition is
satisfied with a margin of roughly eight, at every rotation rate in the
campaign, so the instability under study is the classical
potential-vorticity-gradient one rather than the free-surface energetic route
that \citet{hayashi1987} exhibit on an equatorial $\beta$-plane.

The nondivergent scope of \eqref{eq:genevp} is therefore a deliberate choice, not
an oversight: extending the eigenvalue problem to the divergent system was
investigated and found to be established ground --- \citet{paldor2020} for the
growth-rate comparison, and \citet{white2009} for Ripa's criteria carried onto
the sphere and applied to numerical-model testing.

\section{Instability as counter-propagating Rossby waves}
\label{sec:crw}

This section is the physical heart of the argument. Everything before it has
been either a wave calculation or an instability calculation. Here the two
become the same calculation.

\subsection{An edge wave is Section~\ref{sec:rest} on one line}

Suppose the background potential-vorticity gradient is not spread smoothly over
latitude but concentrated at a single line $y = y_1$, so that
$\dd Q/\dd y = \Delta_1\,\delta(y - y_1)$. This is not an evasion: as
\citet{bretherton1966} pointed out, a discontinuity in the background field is
mathematically equivalent to a delta function in potential vorticity, so the
idealisation is exact for piecewise-constant $Q$.

A meridional displacement $\eta_1$ of that line carries a potential-vorticity
anomaly $q' = -\Delta_1\eta_1$, exactly as in \eqref{eq:zetaeta}. Inverting for
the streamfunction --- ``PV inversion'' in the sense of
\citet{hoskins1985} --- gives a flow that decays away from the line,
%
\begin{equation}
\psi'(y) = -\frac{q'}{2k}\,e^{-k|y-y_1|}\,e^{ikx} ,
\label{eq:edgeinversion}
\end{equation}
%
and the induced meridional velocity at the line itself is again in quadrature
with the displacement. The result is a wave confined to the interface, with
phase speed
%
\begin{equation}
c_1 = \bar u(y_1) - \frac{\Delta_1}{2k} .
\label{eq:edgespeed}
\end{equation}
%
The second term is the intrinsic propagation: to the \emph{left} of the local
potential-vorticity gradient, westward when $Q$ increases poleward, and
inversely proportional to $k$ --- the interface analogue of the $-\beta/k^2$ of
\eqref{eq:betaplane}. An edge wave is the Section~\ref{sec:rest} mechanism
restricted to one line.

\subsection{Two interfaces, and why they can lock}

Now put two such interfaces at $y = \pm b$ with jumps $\Delta_1$, $\Delta_2$ and
local base flows $\bar u_1$, $\bar u_2$. Each wave's inverted flow field
\eqref{eq:edgeinversion} reaches the other, attenuated by $e^{-2kb}$. With
$\gamma_j \equiv \Delta_j/2k$ and $c_j \equiv \bar u_j - \gamma_j$ the isolated
edge-wave speeds, the coupled system reduces to
%
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\;\big(c_1 - c\big)\big(c_2 - c\big)
 = \gamma_1\gamma_2\,e^{-4kb}\;}
\label{eq:crw}
\end{equation}
%
with solutions
%
\begin{equation}
c = \tfrac12(c_1 + c_2) \pm
  \sqrt{\tfrac14(c_1-c_2)^2 + \gamma_1\gamma_2\,e^{-4kb}} .
\label{eq:crwroots}
\end{equation}

Read \eqref{eq:crwroots} physically. Growth requires the discriminant to be
negative. The first term is a square and cannot help, so instability requires
%
\begin{equation}
\gamma_1\gamma_2 < 0
\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad
\Delta_1 \ \text{and}\ \Delta_2 \ \text{of opposite sign} .
\label{eq:oppositesign}
\end{equation}
%
That is the Rayleigh--Kuo sign change of Section~\ref{sec:rayleighkuo}, arrived
at from an entirely different direction: not as an integral identity but as a
condition for two waves to be able to hold station against each other. And once
the sign condition is met, the second requirement is that the coupling
$|\gamma_1\gamma_2|e^{-4kb}$ be large enough to overcome the differential
propagation $\tfrac12|c_1 - c_2|$. This is \emph{phase-locking}: the two edge
waves counter-propagate --- one westward relative to its local flow, one eastward
--- and the shear between them tries to tear them apart. If their intrinsic
counter-propagation exactly offsets the differential advection, they sit at a
fixed relative phase, and at that phase each wave's meridional velocity advects
potential vorticity into the other's crests and troughs, amplifying it. Each
feeds the other and the pair grows exponentially.

\citet{heifetz1999} give the quantitative form of this picture for Rayleigh's
constant-shear strip, tracking the amplitudes and phases of the two waves
explicitly and showing that the growing normal mode is the phase-locked
configuration. Their treatment makes the phase dependence unmistakable: growth
occurs when the northern wave lies less than half a wavelength west of the
southern one, and the pair attenuates when the displacement is the other way.

The window in which both conditions hold is bounded on both sides, and both
bounds are physical:
%
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Short-wave cutoff.} The coupling carries $e^{-2kb}$. A wave much
shorter than the interface separation induces a flow that has decayed to nothing
before it reaches the other interface, so the two waves cannot feel each other
at all.
\item \textbf{Long-wave cutoff.} The intrinsic speeds $\gamma_j \propto 1/k$
diverge as $k\to0$. Very long waves counter-propagate too fast to be held at a
fixed relative phase by the coupling.
\end{itemize}

For Rayleigh's strip, \eqref{eq:crw} reduces exactly to the nondimensional
dispersion relation of \citet[Eq.~(6), p.~2838]{heifetz1999},
$C = \pm(2K)^{-1}\sqrt{(K-1)^2 - e^{-2K}}$ with $K = 2kb$, and returns the
numbers stated on that same page: a cutoff at $K_c = 1.2785$ where
$K_c - 1 = e^{-K_c}$ (against their $\approx 1.28$), an
optimum at $K_m = 0.7968$ (their $\approx 0.8$), and a peak growth rate equal to
$0.2012$ times the shear (their ``about $20\%$''). This is the external validation of the model --- it is an exact reduction
for piecewise-constant potential vorticity, not a cartoon fitted after the fact.

\subsection{What the toy model does and does not predict}

The toy model fixes the mechanism, the sign requirement \eqref{eq:oppositesign},
the existence of both cutoffs, and the \emph{scaling} of the preferred
wavenumber,
%
\begin{equation}
m^{*} \;=\; \frac{K_m\,R\cos\varphi_j}{2\,b_{\mathrm{eff}}} ,
\qquad K_m \approx 0.80 .
\label{eq:mstar}
\end{equation}

It does not fix $b_{\mathrm{eff}}$ for a jet whose potential-vorticity gradient
reverses smoothly over a band rather than jumping at a line, and this must be
stated plainly rather than glossed. For the Galewsky jet, $\dd Q/\dd\varphi$
changes sign four times ($32.2^\circ$, $39.8^\circ$, $49.8^\circ$,
$58.2^\circ$N), giving two reversed-gradient bands flanking a positive core.
Taking $b_{\mathrm{eff}}$ as half the separation between the band centres
($1001$~km) gives $m^{*} \approx 1.8$, against the $m^{*} \approx 6$ that the
solved eigenvalue problem \eqref{eq:galewskygrowth} returns. The two disagree by a
factor of about three, and the disagreement is reported rather than tuned away.
Two reasons are identifiable, both physical: concentrating each gradient into a
delta function maximises the long-range reach of the inverted flow and so biases
the optimum toward longer waves than a distributed gradient supports; and the
Galewsky jet has \emph{three} gradient regions rather than two, so a
two-interface reduction omits one interacting wave entirely.

The standing claims of this section are therefore the mechanism, the sign
requirement, the two cutoffs and the scaling --- not the absolute value of
$m^{*}$, which Section~\ref{sec:evp} supplies.

\subsection{The identification}

\begin{quote}
\textbf{The wave-propagation mechanism of Section~\ref{sec:rest} and the
instability mechanism of Sections~\ref{sec:rayleighkuo}--\ref{sec:evp} are the
same physics, viewed as one potential-vorticity interface versus two.}
\end{quote}

One interface: a displaced column acquires compensating vorticity, the induced
flow is in quadrature with the displacement, and the pattern propagates. Two
interfaces of opposite sign: each does the same thing, but each also sits in the
other's induced flow, and if they can phase-lock, the quadrature that made a
single wave propagate becomes, for the pair, an in-phase forcing that makes both
grow. Nothing new has been added between the two cases except a second
interface.

\medskip
\noindent\emph{Physical payoff:} this is what makes the paper physics-led rather
than a pair of loosely related numerical campaigns.

\section{The Galewsky-type unstable jet and its balanced initial condition}
\label{sec:galewsky}

\subsection{The jet}

The canonical anchor of the instability campaign is the barotropic-instability
test case of \citet{galewsky2004}: a compactly supported, infinitely
differentiable mid-latitude jet,
%
\begin{equation}
\bar u(\varphi) =
\begin{cases}
\dfrac{u_{\max}}{e_n}\,
 \exp\!\left[\dfrac{1}{(\varphi-\varphi_0)(\varphi-\varphi_1)}\right],
 & \varphi_0 < \varphi < \varphi_1,\\[8pt]
0, & \text{otherwise},
\end{cases}
\label{eq:galewskyjet}
\end{equation}
%
with $e_n = \exp[-4/(\varphi_1-\varphi_0)^2]$ chosen so the peak speed is
$u_{\max}$, and with $u_{\max} = 80\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$,
$\varphi_0 = \pi/7$, $\varphi_1 = \pi/2 - \varphi_0$. The compact support is not
cosmetic: because every derivative vanishes at the edges, $\dd Q/\dd y$ from
\eqref{eq:dQdy} tends smoothly to $\beta$ outside the jet and the reversal is
confined to the jet's flanks, with no spurious edge structure to contaminate the
stability analysis.

\subsection{The balanced height field}

A jet imposed on a resting height field is not a solution of anything; it
radiates gravity waves immediately and the instability one then measures is
contaminated by that transient. The initial condition must be \emph{balanced}:
the height field must be the one whose pressure gradient supports the given jet.
For a zonal flow on a sphere the balance is the gradient-wind relation, obtained
by setting $\partial_t = 0$ and $u = \bar u(\varphi)$, $v = 0$ in
\eqref{eq:momv}:
%
\begin{equation}
\frac{1}{R}\frac{\dd (g h)}{\dd\varphi}
 = -\,\bar u(\varphi)\left[f + \frac{\bar u(\varphi)\tan\varphi}{R}\right] ,
\label{eq:gradwind}
\end{equation}
%
the two terms on the right being the Coriolis force on the zonal flow and the
centripetal requirement of following a latitude circle --- the metric term
\eqref{eq:metricterms} in its physical guise. Integrating meridionally,
%
\begin{equation}
g h(\varphi) = g h_0 - \int^{\varphi}
  R\,\bar u(\varphi')\left[f(\varphi')
  + \frac{\bar u(\varphi')\tan\varphi'}{R}\right]\dd\varphi' ,
\label{eq:balint}
\end{equation}
%
with $h_0$ fixed by requiring a prescribed global mean depth (here $10$~km).

An equivalent construction, which is what a spectral solver naturally does,
solves the divergence of the balance relation as a two-dimensional linear
boundary-value problem for $h$ subject to a mean constraint, rather than
integrating \eqref{eq:balint} in one dimension. The two agree to numerical
precision; the project's own comparison of the published construction against
the shipped Dedalus reference implementation is recorded in
\file{tests/phase0_gate/galewsky_comparison.md}, which also identifies the
one genuine difference between them --- the dissipation operator, $\nabla^2$
viscosity in the published case against $\nabla^4$ hyperdiffusion in the
spectral one --- and its consequence, a small shift in instability onset time
that is expected and is not a failure.

\subsection{The perturbation}

The balanced jet is a steady solution and will sit there indefinitely. Growth is
seeded by adding a localised, deliberately \emph{unbalanced} height bump,
%
\begin{equation}
h'(\lambda,\varphi) = \hat h\,\cos\varphi\;
  e^{-(\lambda/\alpha)^2}\;
  e^{-[(\varphi_2-\varphi)/\beta_p]^2} ,
\label{eq:pert}
\end{equation}
%
with $\hat h = 120$~m, $\varphi_2 = \pi/4$, $\alpha = 1/3$, $\beta_p = 1/15$.
Being localised in longitude, it projects onto a broad range of zonal
wavenumbers, so it excites whichever mode \eqref{eq:genevp} says grows fastest
rather than presupposing one; the $\cos\varphi$ factor keeps it well behaved at
the poles.

This section is the analytic groundwork for Session~L5's
\file{initial_conditions/galewsky.py} (run I-00).

\section{Summary: hypotheses to equations}
\label{sec:summary}

Each blueprint hypothesis is mapped to the numbered equation that predicts it and
to the observation that would falsify it.

\begin{longtable}{@{}p{0.05\textwidth}p{0.30\textwidth}p{0.13\textwidth}p{0.42\textwidth}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{ID} & \textbf{Prediction} & \textbf{Equation} & \textbf{Falsified by} \\
\midrule
\endhead
H1 & All initialised modes propagate westward, without exception
   & \eqref{eq:rh}, \eqref{eq:betaplane}
   & Any resolved eastward-propagating barotropic mode. The sign in
     \eqref{eq:betaplane} is unconditional, so a single counter-example
     falsifies the mechanism, not merely the coefficient. \\
H2 & Angular phase speed $\propto 1/[n(n+1)]$ at fixed $\Omega$
   & \eqref{eq:rh}
   & Systematic departure from linearity in $1/[n(n+1)]$ beyond stated
     uncertainty at well-resolved $n$. \\
H3 & Angular phase speed $\propto \Omega$ at fixed $n$
   & \eqref{eq:rh}
   & Nonlinear or saturating dependence on $\Omega$. \\
H4 & Measured speeds agree with $-2\Omega/[n(n+1)]$ to a few per cent at low $n$
   & \eqref{eq:rh}
   & Agreement worse than the quantified numerical uncertainty. \\
H5 & Departure from the nondivergent prediction grows as the wave scale
     approaches $L_d$
   & \eqref{eq:houghevp}, \eqref{eq:limit}
   & No systematic dependence on Burger number; or a measured departure that
     does not converge to zero as $\varepsilon\to0$ at first order. Note the
     sectoral modes $n = m$ depart less than their neighbours for the
     structural reason given in
     Section~\ref{sec:hough:sectoral}; that is predicted, not a failure. \\
H6 & Benchmark error decays spectrally with resolution
   & --- (numerical, not predicted here)
   & Algebraic convergence, indicating an implementation error. \\
H7 & Jets failing the Rayleigh--Kuo condition remain stable
   & \eqref{eq:dQdy}, \eqref{eq:rk}
   & Growth in a run whose base state has no sign change in $\dd Q/\dd y$.
     This is a prohibition, so any single violation falsifies it.
     \textbf{Scoped to modal growth}: the caveat closing
     Section~\ref{sec:evp} applies here. H7 forbids a growing normal mode,
     not finite-time transient amplification, which a non-normal operator
     permits and this project does not test. \\
H8 & Growth rate increases with supercriticality above threshold
   & \eqref{eq:genevp}, \eqref{eq:growth}
   & No systematic relation between shear and growth rate. \\
H9 & Increasing $\Omega$ at fixed jet shape stabilises the flow
   & \eqref{eq:dQdy}, \eqref{eq:genevp}
   & Growth rate independent of, or increasing with, $\Omega$. Physically,
     raising $\Omega$ raises $\beta$ and so makes the curvature term in
     \eqref{eq:dQdy} less able to reverse the sign. \\
H10 & Model dominant zonal wavenumber is of the same order as observed
   & \eqref{eq:genevp}, \eqref{eq:mstar}
   & Order-of-magnitude disagreement in wavenumber. The model side is a
     plateau ($m = 6$--$7$ for the Galewsky jet), not a sharp value, so the
     comparison is band-against-band. \\
\bottomrule
\end{longtable}

\noindent
Two entries deserve a note. H6 is a statement about the numerical method, not
about the physics, and is deliberately not derived here. H10 is supported by two
independent routes --- the solved eigenvalue problem \eqref{eq:genevp} and the
counter-propagating-wave scaling \eqref{eq:mstar} --- which currently disagree by
a factor of about three for the Galewsky jet; Section~\ref{sec:crw} states why,
and the eigenvalue problem is the one to be trusted. Note also that the
eigenvalue problem returns a plateau spanning $m = 6$--$7$ rather than a single
wavenumber, so H10 is tested band-against-band.
